Echo Chambers in Recommender Systems

Do recommender algorithms narrow what users see, and what does a simulation need before it can tell?

In short

The early results looked clean, and every one of them turned out to be a bug. Once the simulation was fixed, the three standard algorithms were no different from random. The real contribution is knowing why, and what a simulation needs before it can detect an echo chamber at all.

Research overview

This project asked whether recommender algorithms (popularity-based, content-based and collaborative filtering) gradually narrow what users see, creating an “echo chamber”. We tested this in a simulation: 100 MovieLens users, each receiving ten recommendations per round, “clicking” according to their genre preferences, with that behaviour fed back into the system. We tracked genre diversity and Shannon entropy over time to compare how each algorithm behaved.

Echo chambers get blamed for political polarisation and radicalisation online, and for people discovering less new content. But much of that is assumed rather than tested. I wanted to see whether the effect really shows up under controlled conditions, and what you would need to measure it properly.

The early results looked clean: collaborative filtering broadened exposure, content-based filtering narrowed it, and popularity swung widely. Every one of those turned out to be a bug in the simulation rather than a real effect. Once we had found and corrected the flaws, the three original algorithms showed no difference from random recommendation.

Approach

(01) Control

Simulate, against random

A simulation let us isolate mechanisms under controlled conditions. Alongside the three baseline algorithms we added a random-recommendation control, so changes were compared against noise rather than against zero.

(02) Check

Could the design show an effect?

When the corrected baseline showed nothing, I tested the design itself. Content-based filtering at click rates 100× apart gave bit-identical recommendations, so it had no feedback loop. Collaborative filtering’s signal came from neighbouring users, not the individual.

(03) Redesign

Separate taste from the guess

No algorithm both read one user’s preferences and updated from that user’s behaviour. So I split each user’s true taste (which drives clicks, hidden from the algorithm) from the algorithm’s inferred profile, and built an adaptive content-based algorithm whose clicks update only the inferred profile.

Evaluation: five defects

Each defect was isolated with a diagnostic and confirmed with a controlled comparison.

  1. 01

    Shared rating matrix. Users’ simulated clicks leaked into later users’ collaborative-filtering calculations. This created the whole +1.57-genre “broadening”; giving each user their own copy made it vanish.

  2. 02

    Inconsistent exclusion. Popularity and content-based filtering were recommending films users had already rated (17.8% and 3.3% of recommendations).

  3. 03

    Profiles measuring sparsity, not taste. Preference concentration correlated with rating count at ρ = +0.715. Rebuilding profiles from viewing proportions made the click model about 3.4× better at telling a user’s top genre apart from the rest.

  4. 04

    Unrealistic click rate. Click rate had been set equal to the affinity score, giving 49–69% against a realistic 1–5%, so we recalibrated it.

  5. 05

    Biased tie-breaking. Ties went to the film with the higher ID, which favours newer films. This affected 91–97% of content-based picks.

After all that, there was one real effect. The adaptive algorithm narrowed genre variety significantly more than the fixed one, by about 1.3 genres, and it was the only version that clearly beat the random baseline. Users saw more different films, but from fewer genres, and the narrowing levelled off after about 30 rounds instead of spiralling.

My role

I was the team’s research mentor, so checking that the work was valid was my job. When collaborative filtering kept trending upward and popularity was jumping around too much to be believable, I did not just accept the explanations my teammates gave me. I audited the code myself and walked them through each bug as it came to light.

From there I directed the investigation, deciding what to test and which results not to trust, and I caught mistakes in my own analysis too. I made the main design calls: separating true taste from the algorithm’s guess, building the adaptive algorithm and adding the random baseline. I also helped my teammates explain their parts of the project, and wrote up the evaluation for our CREST Silver report.
